Why the origin of meat affects its climate impact

South American beef weighs more

Not all beef is equal

Beef is the meat with the highest climate impact. But how high it is varies greatly from country to country. Worldwide, two farms making the same product can differ by up to fifty times. So where your meat comes from makes a big difference.

Why does European beef score better?

  • Much European beef comes from dairy cattle. Around 80% of beef in Europe comes from dairy farming: from cows that first gave milk, or from calves born so that their mother would keep producing milk. Because these animals provide two products, the emissions are shared between milk and meat. Beef from animals kept only for their meat carries all the emissions itself.
  • Good nutrition and animal health. European cattle get more digestible feed, so they emit less methane per kilo of meat. They grow more steadily, are healthier and fewer of them die. As a result, fewer animals are needed to produce the same amount of meat.
  • Stricter rules and extra efforts. Flanders has a strict manure policy. Together with changed manure management and a smaller herd, this cut the greenhouse gas emissions of Flemish agriculture by a quarter between 1990 and 2008. The Flemish cattle sector has also committed to reducing methane emissions from cows, among other things through adapted feed.

Why does South American beef score worse?

In South America, cattle are more often kept only for their meat, and forest is still being cleared to make room for pasture. A forest stores a lot of carbon: when it disappears, that carbon is released as CO₂. In Latin America, deforestation accounts for about a third of the emissions from beef.

How to read this table

Researchers often calculate per kilo of carcass: the slaughtered animal without head, hide and innards, but with bones and fat. On average, about 70% of a carcass becomes saleable meat. Per kilo of meat, emissions are therefore higher than per kilo of carcass. The last column is a rough estimate of that. What matters most is the difference between regions: Latin American beef is about three to four times higher than European beef.

Why do these values differ from ClimateCart?

The figures in ClimateCart come from Agribalyse, a French database. It follows a product from the farm all the way to your home. Besides raising the animal, Agribalyse therefore also counts transport, packaging, refrigeration in the shop and what gets lost along the way. That is why the values in the app are somewhat higher than in this table, which only counts up to the meat itself. Both ways of calculating are correct: they simply answer a different question.

And pork?

For pork, the differences are smaller, because pigs don't live on pasture. Most of their climate impact (about two thirds in Flanders) comes from their feed. Here too, Belgium scores well:

Brazil scores higher mainly for two reasons. Part of the animal feed there is grown on land that was recently forest or savannah. And in a warm climate, more methane is released when manure is stored. How big the difference is exactly depends on how researchers count that deforestation: other studies find a smaller difference.

Flemish pigs also eat soy from South America. The Belgian animal feed sector aims to have all imported soy certified as sustainable by 2030.

How do you know where your meat comes from?

In the EU, fresh and frozen beef must always state its origin: where the animal was born, reared and slaughtered. For processed products, such as lasagne or burgers in sauce, this is not mandatory.

What does this mean for you?

  • If you eat beef, choose Belgian or European meat and take a quick look at the label.
  • But even the most efficient beef remains far heavier than chicken, pork or plant-based proteins. Eating less beef is still the biggest win.
